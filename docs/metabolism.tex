\title{\textbf{Recovering reaction kinetics and homeostatic regulation from
metabolite dynamics with graph neural networks}}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}
\maketitle
\begin{abstract}
A metabolic network is a bipartite graph in which metabolites are coupled
through reactions, each with its own rate constant and stoichiometry. We
pose the \emph{inverse} problem: given only the time series of metabolite
concentrations and the bipartite graph structure, recover the components
that generated the dynamics --- the per-reaction rate constants $k_j$, the
substrate-to-rate kinetic law, and the per-metabolite homeostatic
regulation. We replace the unknown functions of the forward model with
learnable MLPs that operate on the metabolite--reaction graph and a vector
of learnable rate constants, and train the graph neural network (GNN)
end-to-end to predict $dc/dt$. On synthetic mass-action systems with known
ground truth we study rate-constant recovery and the identifiability
obstacles that arise from scale ambiguity, functional compensation, and
homeostasis absorption. We outline the path from synthetic data to real
metabolomics --- starting from fully parameterised kinetic models from
BioModels (yeast glycolysis), constraint-based genome-scale models
(yeast-GEM), and high-frequency real-time metabolomics during nutrient
transitions. This is a companion methods note to our connectome-dynamics
studies, which apply the same sign-/structure-locked GNN inverse-modelling
recipe to neural circuits.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Cellular metabolism is a large, sparse reaction network: hundreds of
metabolites are interconverted by hundreds of enzyme-catalysed reactions,
each proceeding at a rate set by an intrinsic rate constant and the
instantaneous concentrations of its substrates. The \emph{forward} problem
--- predicting concentration dynamics given the network, the kinetics and
the rate constants --- is classical biochemistry. The \emph{inverse}
problem is harder and is our subject: given measured concentration
trajectories and the graph of which metabolites enter which reactions,
recover the rate constants and the functional form of the kinetics. This
is the metabolic analogue of the connectome-to-dynamics inverse problems
we study elsewhere, and it shares their central difficulty: the mapping
from parameters to dynamics is nonlinear, high-dimensional, and degenerate
--- many parameter combinations produce nearly identical trajectories.

\section{Metabolic networks as bipartite graphs}
\label{sec:graph}

A metabolic network is naturally a \textbf{bipartite graph} with two node
types: metabolites and reactions. Edges connect only nodes of different
type, and each edge carries an integer \emph{stoichiometric coefficient}.
A single-partite metabolite--metabolite graph cannot represent the system,
because a reaction couples \emph{several} substrates and products at once
through a shared rate constant: collapsing it to pairwise metabolite edges
would lose the information that those metabolites participate in the same
reaction.

\paragraph{Stoichiometric matrix.}
The connectivity is summarised by the stoichiometric matrix
$\mathbf{S} \in \R^{n \times m}$ ($n$ metabolites, $m$ reactions), where
$S_{ij}$ is the signed coefficient with which metabolite $i$ enters
reaction $j$: negative for substrates (consumed), positive for products
(produced). $\mathbf{S}$ is sparse (each reaction touches only a handful of
metabolites), integer-valued (typically in $\{-2,-1,0,+1,+2\}$), and for
mass-balanced reactions its columns sum to zero.

\section{Forward model: reaction kinetics and homeostasis}
\label{sec:forward}

\paragraph{Mass-action kinetics.}
Under the law of mass action a reaction proceeds faster when its substrates
are more abundant, with each substrate raised to the power of its
stoichiometric coefficient. For reaction $j$ with rate constant $k_j$,
\begin{equation}
v_j \;=\; k_j \prod_{k \in \mathrm{sub}(j)} c_k^{\,|S_{kj}|},
\label{eq:massaction}
\end{equation}
so a reaction consuming two molecules of a substrate depends quadratically
on its concentration, and one consuming a single molecule depends linearly.
The product over substrates (\emph{multiplicative} aggregation) encodes the
requirement that \emph{all} substrates be present simultaneously, and the
resulting nonlinear coupling is what allows autocatalytic cycles to sustain
oscillations. A simpler \emph{additive} aggregation
$v_j = k_j \sum_{k} c_k^{|S_{kj}|}$ treats substrates as contributing
independently and is a useful surrogate for enzyme-mediated reactions that
are not strictly mass-action.

\paragraph{Full dynamics.}
Combining reaction fluxes through $\mathbf{S}$ with a per-metabolite
homeostatic restoring force gives the complete forward model
\begin{equation}
\frac{dc_i}{dt}
  = \underbrace{-\lambda_i\,(c_i - c_i^{\mathrm{baseline}})}_{\text{homeostasis}}
  + \underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^{m} S_{ij}\, v_j}_{\text{reaction dynamics}},
\label{eq:full}
\end{equation}
with $v_j$ from \cref{eq:massaction}. The homeostatic term pulls each
metabolite toward a baseline level $c_i^{\mathrm{baseline}}$ at a
metabolite-specific rate $\lambda_i$; crucially it is \emph{small} relative
to the reaction fluxes, which is what makes it hard to recover
(\cref{sec:identifiability}). \Cref{eq:full} spans a family of regimes used
for synthetic data: pure reaction ($\lambda = 0$, additive), homeostatic
($\lambda > 0$, additive, convergent), and oscillatory (autocatalytic
3-cycles, multiplicative, sustained oscillations).

\section{Inverse problem: the GNN parameterisation}
\label{sec:inverse}

Given observed trajectories $\{c_i(t)\}$ and the bipartite graph, we
recover the generating model by replacing every unknown function in
\cref{eq:full,eq:massaction} with a learnable component on the graph:
\begin{equation}
\frac{dc_i}{dt}
  = \underbrace{\mlpnode(c_i, a_i)}_{\approx\,-\lambda_i(c_i - c_i^{\mathrm{baseline}})}
  + \sum_{j=1}^{m} S_{ij}
    \underbrace{k_j \prod_{k \in \mathrm{sub}(j)} \mlpsub\!\big(c_k, |S_{kj}|\big)}
              _{\approx\,k_j\,c_k^{|S_{kj}|}}.
\label{eq:gnn}
\end{equation}
The GNN operates on the metabolite--reaction graph by message passing:
substrate states are mapped through $\mlpsub$, aggregated per reaction
(multiplicatively for mass-action), scaled by the learnable rate constant
$k_j$, and distributed back to metabolites through $\mathbf{S}$; the
node update $\mlpnode$ adds the self-regulation term. Training minimises the
prediction error on $dc/dt$ end-to-end. The learnable parameters are
listed in \cref{tab:params}.

\paragraph{Function discovery.}
The GNN is not told the kinetic law. $\mlpsub$ receives a substrate
concentration and its stoichiometric coefficient and must learn the
power-law response $c_k^{|S_{kj}|}$ from data alone --- in particular it
must distinguish linear ($c^1$) from quadratic ($c^2$) dependence as the
coefficient changes. Likewise $\mlpnode$ must recover the subtle,
small-magnitude homeostatic signal without absorbing dynamics that belong
to the reaction terms.

\section{Identifiability}
\label{sec:identifiability}

The three learnable components interact and can compensate for one another,
producing degenerate solutions with high prediction accuracy but poor
parameter recovery:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Scale ambiguity.} The product $k_j\,\mlpsub$ is invariant
    under $k \to \alpha k,\ \mlpsub \to \mlpsub/\alpha$. Without anchoring,
    the MLP absorbs a global scale and shifts every $k_j$. A centring
    regulariser pins $\mathrm{mean}(\log k)$ to the known range centre.
  \item \textbf{Functional compensation.} If $\mlpsub$ settles on a wrong
    exponent (\eg{} $c^{1.5}$ instead of $c^2$), the rate constants can
    partly compensate by re-scaling, masking the error.
  \item \textbf{Homeostasis absorption.} $\mlpnode$ can grow and soak up
    dynamics that should be explained by reactions, biasing the recovered
    rate constants.
\end{itemize}
These are the metabolic counterparts of the sign/scale degeneracies that
motivate the structure-locking and regularisation priors in our
connectome-dynamics models.

\section{Toy model}
\label{sec:toy}

The toy model is a synthetic rank-50 oscillatory mass-action network ($100$
metabolites, $256$ reactions, stoichiometry $\mathbf{S}$ given, no homeostasis),
generated with known rate constants and fit leak-free (the loss reads only the
concentration dynamics, never a ground-truth label). The \emph{rate-constant
recovery} $R^2$ (learned vs.\ true $\log_{10} k_j$) is $0.75 \pm 0.04$ across
four seeds (\cref{tab:cv}); for the published seed it is raw $0.74$ over all
$256$ reactions, or trimmed $0.98$ after excluding the $4.3\%$ ($11/256$)
outliers, at slope $1.00$ (\cref{fig:toy_dashboard}c). The two learned functions
match ground truth (\cref{fig:toy_dashboard}a,b): $\mathrm{MLP_{sub}}$ recovers the substrate
law $c^{|s|}$ and $\mathrm{MLP_{node}}$ (homeostasis) stays $\approx 0$, the
correct value for this regime. Rollout quality is measured by the \emph{Pearson}
correlation between the learned free autoregressive rollout and the true
trajectory: it is high early ($0.96$ up to time $25$) and degrades as
integration error accumulates ($0.85$ at time $50$, $0.74$ up to the divergence
onset at time ${\approx}124$), after which the rollout diverges
(\cref{fig:toy_dashboard}d). The rate constants are recovered, but single-step
training places no constraint on multi-step stability, so the autocatalytic
dynamics eventually blow up. Rate-constant recovery and rollout are different
questions: this model recovers the parameters but is not a stable simulator.

We then asked whether a recurrent training curriculum closes the stability gap:
the rollout horizon is grown across epochs (single-step, then $100, 200, \dots,
1000$ integration steps, with a co-ramped learning rate and gradient clipping).
A naive run is a cautionary tale about the metric. The free trajectory no longer
diverges (\cref{fig:toy_recurrent}d), and the \emph{pooled} rollout Pearson even
rises ($0.74 \to 0.83$) --- but the pooled correlation is dominated by
\emph{between-metabolite} level differences and merely rewards staying bounded.
Measured honestly \emph{per metabolite} (each rollout against its own true
trajectory), fidelity \emph{collapses} from $0.47$ (single-step) to $0.18$: the
curriculum trades the single-step model's correct-but-unstable oscillations for a
smooth, bounded, dynamically dead trajectory that no longer reproduces the peaks.
Rate-constant recovery collapses in step (\cref{fig:toy_recurrent}c): the
correlation between learned and true $\log_{10} k_j$ falls from $0.87$ to $0.07$
(raw $R^2 = 0.004$, $90\%$ outliers), and the homeostasis $\mathrm{MLP_{node}}$,
correctly $\approx 0$ under single-step training, drifts to a spurious nonzero
function. The multi-step objective admits a smooth \emph{degenerate} solution ---
wrong rate constants and a spurious homeostasis term reproduce a damped trajectory
without the mechanism --- the same right-dynamics/wrong-parameters degeneracy seen
on glycolysis. The lesson is twofold: report \emph{per-metabolite} rollout
fidelity (the pooled number is flattering), and recognise that the naive
curriculum does not buy stability so much as suppress dynamics. Whether \emph{any}
curriculum achieves a genuinely good rollout (high per-metabolite fidelity
\emph{and} stability \emph{and} preserved $k$-recovery) is an open question we are
addressing with a sweep of schemes --- horizon caps, learning-rate decay,
$k$-anchoring (freezing the recovered rate constants past the single-step warmup),
soft tail losses, and longer warmups; results pending.

A different lever closes the stability gap where the curriculum could not:
supplying a known \emph{external stimulus}. The toy is autonomous, and a long
autonomous rollout of a linear oscillatory system collapses to a damped solution
under any multi-step objective (above). Feeding a known time-varying drive to a
subset of metabolites at every rollout step --- the boundary-input convention used
by the calcium models --- changes the picture. With the drive supplied and ten
distinct driven trajectories for training, even \emph{single-step} training yields
a rollout that is stable over the full window (no divergence) while preserving
rate-constant recovery ($R^2 = 0.78$), and it stays bounded on a held-out
trajectory with a different drive (\cref{fig:toy_stim}). The external input anchors
the dynamics the way it does in the connectome models. Per-metabolite fidelity
($0.33$) does not yet exceed the stimulus-free early-window value ($0.47$), but the
stimulus model holds that fidelity across the \emph{entire} trajectory rather than
only up to divergence; whether the curriculum, now anchored by the drive, lifts it
further without the degeneracy was then tested directly, by running the
stimulus-driven \emph{curriculum} (three horizon schedules, each to a fixed $12$\,h
compute budget; \cref{fig:toy_stim_rec}). The answer is negative: the curriculum does
not improve on single-step training. Among the three schedules the best $k$-recovery
comes from the \emph{capped} horizon (cap-$100$): the rollout stays stable and
dynamically alive over the full window (per-metabolite Pearson $0.31$), so the
stimulus does prevent the dead-collapse that the stimulus-free curriculum suffered
(per-metabolite $\le 0.23$, $k$ destroyed). But the rollout fidelity ($0.31$) merely
matches single-step training ($0.33$, \cref{fig:toy_stim}) rather than exceeding it,
\emph{and} rate-constant recovery degrades --- from $R^2 = 0.78$ / $4\%$ outliers
(single-step) to $R^2 = 0.44$ / $14\%$ outliers, now failing the $\le 10\%$ gate. The
uncapped and zebrafish-style schedules are worse still on $k$ ($32\%$ and $84\%$
outliers). The earlier warm-up reading of a high $k$-recovery held only at the short
horizon; once training actually reaches a multi-step horizon the curriculum erodes
$k$, exactly the stability/identifiability tension of \cref{fig:toy_recurrent},
merely softened (not removed) by the drive. \textbf{Single-step training with the
external stimulus remains the best toy configuration.}

\textbf{Intrinsic noise as an identifiability lever --- and the right form for a
conserved system.} Stochastic forcing \emph{inside} the dynamics (distinct from
measurement noise on the observations) can diversify a trajectory and, in a
degenerate problem, help break identifiability. But the \emph{form} of the noise
matters when the state obeys a conservation law. The obvious choice --- an additive
term $\sigma\,\xi_i(t)$ on the concentrations themselves, i.e.\ integrating the
stochastic differential equation $dc = f(c)\,dt + \sigma\,dW$ by its Euler step --- is
stoichiometrically \emph{unsound}: it injects or removes mass that no reaction
produced, violating conservation, so it can only corrupt the trajectory rather than
realise a physical one. We swept it regardless (\cref{fig:toy_noise}), training
single-step on the \emph{noisy} trajectory and evaluating against the matched
\emph{noise-free} twin (same network and seed). Three findings. (i)~The injected noise does diversify the
data: activity rank rises monotonically with $\sigma$ ($35 \to 47$). (ii)~But
rate-constant recovery is merely \emph{robust}, not improved: for $\sigma \le 0.05$
the trimmed $R^2$ holds at $0.98$--$0.99$ with $5$--$6\%$ outliers (raw $R^2$
fluctuates $0.60$--$0.78$, within single-draw seed variation), and at $\sigma = 0.07$
it \emph{collapses} ($95\%$ outliers) as the process noise corrupts the trajectory
itself. (iii)~The deterministic model trained on the noisy data partially reproduces
the noise-free dynamics it never saw cleanly (\cref{fig:toy_noise}, top row: the
black prediction stays smooth while the green ground truth it is scored against grows
visibly noisier with $\sigma$) but --- like every single-step model here --- does not
become a faithful long-horizon simulator.
The stoichiometrically sound alternative perturbs the reaction \emph{rates} rather
than the state --- $k_j(t) = k_j\,(1 + \sigma\,\xi_j(t))$, a fluctuation in enzyme
activity. Each reaction then still conserves mass through $S$ and only its flux
varies, so every trajectory stays physical (sub-$10\%$ mass drift) while the data
diversify as much as before (activity rank $35 \to 50$ at $\sigma = 0.3$). Trained
single-step on the rate-noisy data, $k$-recovery tells the same story as the additive
form: \emph{benign then harmful}. At low noise it is unchanged from $\sigma = 0$
($\sigma = 0.1$: trimmed $R^2 = 0.96$, $4.7\%$ outliers --- the raw $R^2$ of $0.85$
sits inside the cross-seed band, with no gain in the bulk fit), and it degrades
monotonically as the noise grows ($\sigma = 0.3$: $21\%$ outliers; $\sigma = 0.5$:
$60\%$). So getting the noise \emph{physically right} does not change the outcome:
with $S$ given, $k$ is \emph{already} identifiable at $\sigma = 0$
(\cref{fig:toy_dashboard}), so there is no degeneracy left for any noise to break.
The lever would matter only in a genuinely $k$-degenerate regime (e.g.\ $S$ unknown),
which we leave to future work.

\section{Toward real metabolomics}
\label{sec:realdata}

The framework is developed and validated on synthetic data where ground
truth is known; the goal is real metabolomic time series. Three concrete
datasets, now vendored in the repository, stage that transition from fully
parameterised kinetics, through genome-scale topology, to a real recording.
None of the three is solved yet; this section describes what each provides
and what stands between it and a result.

\paragraph{Rung 1 --- yeast glycolysis kinetic model (BioModels
the published yeast glycolysis kinetic model).}
A small, fully parameterised kinetic model of glycolysis in
\emph{S.\ cerevisiae} \citep{smallbone2013}, imported from SBML. After
separating dynamic from boundary species it gives $20$ internal metabolites
($+9$ fixed boundary species), $30$ reactions including iso-enzymes (three
hexokinase, three pyruvate-decarboxylase, three glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate
variants), with real stoichiometry $\mathbf{S}$ and measured initial
concentrations. The stoichiometry is sparse ($77$ nonzero entries) and almost
entirely unit-valued --- coefficients are $\pm 1$ ($40$ negative, $35$ positive)
with a single $-2$ and a single $-3$ --- but, unlike the all-$|s|{=}1$ toy, it is
genuinely \emph{multi-substrate}: of the $30$ reactions $15$ are first-order in
substrate, $13$ are second-order ($|s|{=}2$), and one is fourth-order, so there is
real kinetic curvature to recover. Crucially the rate laws are \emph{not}
mass-action: they
mix reversible/irreversible Michaelis--Menten, Hill, and allosteric forms,
so $v_j = V_{\max,j}\prod_k\!\big[c_k/(K_{m,kj}+c_k)\big]^{|S_{kj}|}$ rather
than a power law. This is the cleanest available ground truth: known
$V_{\max}$/$K_m$ let us score parameter recovery directly. \emph{Status:}
the SBML import, $\mathbf{S}$ extraction, and a Michaelis--Menten simulator
are in place and generate a $12{,}000$-frame dataset, and we port the
connectome-style \emph{autoregressive curriculum} (per-epoch horizon ramp
$10\to300$, soft tail-loss, LR co-ramp, gradient clipping) to stabilise the
rollout. Two cautions, both found by being sceptical of an initially good
number. First, the test rollout looked catastrophic ($R^2={-}390$) only because
the evaluator omitted the model's \emph{boundary stimulus}; supplying it, the
curriculum model reproduces the trajectory well (global $R^2 = 0.99$,
\cref{fig:glyco_rollout}). Second, that success is misleading: it is
\emph{in-sample} (a single trajectory, no held-out run), the per-metabolite
$R^2$ is only $0.15$, and the leak-resistant test fails outright --- the
recovered $V_{max}$ correlate with ground truth at $R^2 \approx 0$ (slope
$0.06$, $73\%$ outliers, \cref{fig:glyco_recovery}). The model therefore
reproduces the dynamics with the \emph{wrong} kinetics: a degenerate fit. A
\emph{direct held-out test} confirms it does not generalise: rolling the trained
model out on an unseen trajectory (a different boundary stimulus, comparable
rank, \cref{fig:glyco_holdout}) collapses the fit from global Pearson $0.997$
in-sample to $0.28$ (global $R^2$ $0.99 \to 0.05$; per-metabolite $R^2$ strongly
negative) --- the predicted traces settle to the wrong levels and no longer track
the true dynamics. The good in-sample rollout was effectively memorisation of the
single training trajectory. The curriculum fixed rollout \emph{stability}; the real
open problem on real Michaelis--Menten data is parameter \emph{identifiability}
--- the same scale/compensation degeneracy as the synthetic setup, but here
unbroken. (One robust falsification survived both metrics: the config with the
lowest \emph{training} loss had the worst rollout \emph{and} the worst recovery,
so training loss is not a proxy for either.)

\paragraph{Can $\mathrm{MLP_{sub}}$ learn the kinetic curvature?}
A natural suspect for the recovery failures is that the MLP cannot represent the
substrate power law $c^{|s|}$. On the synthetic toy this is untestable --- that
network is $100\%$ $|s|{=}1$ (linear), so any probe at $|s|{\ge}2$ is untrained
extrapolation. We therefore built a controlled regime (the $|s|{\ge}2$ regime: $31\%$
of substrate edges with $|s|{\ge}2$) and compared a plain MLP against the
log-space parameterisation $g=\exp(\mathrm{MLP}(\log c, |s|))$
(\cref{fig:curvature}). Two things hold. First, \emph{with} genuine $|s|{\ge}2$
signal the MLP now learns \emph{some} curvature (the toy could not), and log-space
learns more: the growth of $\mathrm{MLP_{sub}}$ over $c\in[1,9]$ reaches
$24\!\to\!32$ for $c^2$ (true $81$) and $33\!\to\!75$ for $c^4$ (true $6561$),
plain $\to$ log-space. So curvature \emph{does} matter and log-space helps. Second,
\emph{both still badly undershoot} the higher powers, and both fail the
$10\%$-outlier gate on this regime (raw $k$ $R^2 = 0.16$ plain vs $0.26$
log-space; ${\sim}40\%$ outliers). The clearest gain from log-space is dynamical
rather than in parameter recovery: the convergent-window rollout Pearson rises
from $0.27$ (plain) to $0.76$ (log-space), because matching the curvature where
the data lives stabilises the integration even when the steep exponents are not
fully recovered. Representing $c^2$--$c^4$ over a wide range
remains hard for either parameterisation --- a real but partial fix.

\paragraph{The limit is signal, not capacity.} Two further controls localise the
residual failure. (i)~Training on four diverse trajectories instead of one
barely helps (outliers $37.5\%\!\to\!32\%$), so it is not a data-volume /
identifiability-by-replication problem. (ii)~A \emph{structured} substrate
function $g=c^{a(|s|)}$ --- which can represent any exponent \emph{exactly} ---
still fails (outliers $30.9\%$): its learned exponent map saturates at
$a(s){=}1.0, 1.45, 1.64, 1.71, 1.73, 1.75$ for $s{=}1{\dots}6$, against the true
$1,2,3,4,5,6$. It nails $a(1)$ ($499$ reactions) but never reaches the high
exponents --- because those reactions are \emph{rare} ($15/12/8/2$ reactions at
$|s|{=}3/4/5/6$). The unrecoverable ${\sim}31\%$ are exactly the $|s|{\ge}2$
reactions, across all three parameterisations. So the bottleneck for high-order
kinetics is neither MLP capacity nor data volume but the \emph{scarcity of
high-order reactions}: too little signal to identify a steep exponent. The fix is
a structural prior (e.g.\ integer exponents) or a regime richer in high-$|s|$
reactions --- not a better generic function approximator.

\paragraph{Rung 2 --- consensus genome-scale model (yeast-GEM).}
The community-curated genome-scale model of \emph{S.\ cerevisiae}
\citep{yeastgem}: $2{,}806$ metabolites, $4{,}131$ reactions across $14$
compartments. It supplies a large, real stoichiometric matrix $\mathbf{S}$
and flux bounds but \emph{no} kinetic rate laws --- the true inverse
problem. The intended use is to extract a central-carbon subgraph
(glycolysis $+$ TCA $+$ fermentation, ${\sim}100$--$150$ reactions), drive
it with dynamic-FBA or literature kinetics to generate in-silico dynamics,
and fit the GNN in ``S given'' mode on a realistic topology. \emph{Status:}
not started beyond vendoring the model; subgraph extraction and a dynamics
generator are still to be written.

\paragraph{Rung 3 --- real-time metabolomics during nutrient transitions
(real data).}
High-frequency flow-injection mass spectrometry of live microbial culture
\citep{link2015}, now downloaded as the paper's supplementary tables. For
\emph{E.\ coli} the released data give a metabolite$\times$time intensity
matrix of $247$ annotated ions over $119$ evenly spaced time points in
three biological replicates (a long batch course), plus a dedicated
starvation$\to$growth switch series of $302$ ions over ${\sim}148$ points;
companion \emph{B.\ subtilis} and \emph{Y.\ lipolytica} sets are included.
Each ion carries a KEGG annotation ($444$ distinct KEGG IDs for
\emph{E.\ coli}), though many ions are mass-ambiguous (one $m/z$ maps to
several candidate metabolites) and values are \emph{relative} ion
intensities, not absolute concentrations. \emph{Status:} the data are in
hand but not yet ingested; the work is to deduplicate/annotate ions, build
$\mathbf{S}$ for the measured subset from an \emph{E.\ coli} genome-scale
model (\eg{} iJO1366) via KEGG$\to$BiGG mapping, and rescale the
relative intensities. There is \emph{no} kinetic ground truth here, so
success can only be judged by held-out prediction, not by recovered $k_j$.
A parallel mammalian direction is chronic jugular microdialysis in freely
moving mice \citep{nardin2025} (${\sim}123$ metabolites every $7.5$~min over
${\sim}8$~h; $10$ components explain $73\%$ of variance).

\paragraph{What transfers and what changes.}
The core recipe transfers: the bipartite graph, message passing over
reactions, function discovery via $\mlpsub/\mlpnode$, and log-space
rate-constant recovery. Real data adds, all at once, the obstacles the
synthetic setup omits: non-mass-action (saturating, allosteric) kinetics
that $\mlpsub$ must learn; a partially known and incomplete $\mathbf{S}$
(only measured metabolites, ambiguous identities); two orders of magnitude
fewer, unevenly spaced time points ($\sim 10^2$ vs $\sim 10^3$); relative
rather than absolute concentrations; and external drivers (the nutrient
switch) that must enter as a term $f_{\mathrm{ext}}(t)$ in \cref{eq:full}.

\paragraph{What we expect.}
We do \emph{not} expect the real-data rungs to work soon. The realistic
near-term target is Rung~1: get the GNN to recover the published
$V_{\max}$/$K_m$ of a real kinetic model under Michaelis--Menten kinetics
--- a contained problem with ground truth, but one the current code does not
yet solve. Rung~2 is a topology-realism check with no kinetic ground truth.
Rung~3 (real \emph{E.\ coli}) is the genuine goal but also where every
difficulty above appears together; a first ``result'' there means only
predicting held-out frames of measured dynamics, not recovering mechanism.
\Cref{tab:status} summarises the gap. The honest reading: one validated
synthetic working point, three real rungs at the data-and-scaffolding stage,
and substantial method development (saturating kinetics, honest homeostasis,
incomplete-network handling, relative-concentration rescaling) still
required before any real-data claim.

\subsection{First look: are the four real datasets amenable?}
\label{sec:amenability}

Before training, we run the same cheap test on \emph{all four} datasets
measured by \citet{link2015} --- \emph{E.\ coli}, \emph{B.\ subtilis},
\emph{Y.\ lipolytica}, and a hybrid sample --- asking whether each recording
carries the structure the GNN needs. The identical four-probe analysis
is applied to every organism: SVD low-rank,
per-ion lag-1 autocorrelation, example traces, and KEGG coverage against a
common reference network (iJO1366; a proxy for core-metabolite
mappability --- exact for \emph{E.\ coli}, approximate for the others). For the
\emph{Y.\ lipolytica} fit target the proper yeast network yeast-GEM
raises coverage from $63\%$ to $77\%$ ($127/164$ ions), confirming the
under-estimate from the bacterial proxy.
\Cref{tab:amenability} summarises and \cref{fig:amenability_grid} shows the
per-organism panels.

\paragraph{What the three probes compute, and how to read them.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{SVD low-rank.} Stack the recording into a matrix
    $C \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times T}$ ($N$ ions, $T$ time points) and $z$-score
    each row over time, so the probe sees temporal \emph{shape} not absolute
    scale. Its singular values $\sigma_1 \ge \sigma_2 \ge \dots$ give the
    variance carried by each independent mode, $\sigma_i^2 / \sum_j \sigma_j^2$;
    $\mathrm{rank}_{99}$ is the smallest number of modes whose cumulative
    variance reaches $99\%$. \emph{Interpretation:} it counts the effective
    dimensionality of the dynamics. A small $\mathrm{rank}_{99}$ relative to $N$
    means the metabolites move in a coordinated, low-dimensional way --- shared
    drivers and strong correlations --- which is exactly the structure a compact
    network model can fit; $\mathrm{rank}_{99}\!\approx\!N$ would mean every ion
    moves independently and no parsimonious mechanism exists. We compare the
    \emph{fraction} $\mathrm{rank}_{99}/N$ against the synthetic regime
    ($\sim$45\%).
  \item \textbf{Per-ion lag-1 autocorrelation.} For each ion's mean-subtracted
    trace $x(t)$, $\rho_1 = \sum_t x(t)\,x(t{-}1) \big/ \sum_t x(t)^2$ --- the
    correlation of the signal with itself shifted by one frame; we report the
    median over ions and the fraction with $\rho_1 > 0.5$.
    \emph{Interpretation:} it measures temporal smoothness. $\rho_1 \to 1$ means
    consecutive samples are nearly equal --- a smooth, slowly varying trace
    whose finite-difference derivative $dc/dt$ is well defined and carries
    signal; $\rho_1 \to 0$ means white-noise-like, so the measured $dc/dt$ is
    dominated by noise. This is the decisive test for our setting, since the GNN
    learns $dc/dt = f(c)$: a derivative made of noise cannot be fit, however
    low-rank or well-annotated the data are.
  \item \textbf{KEGG coverage.} Each ion is annotated with one or more KEGG
    compound identifiers from its $m/z$ (an ion matching several formulae is
    \emph{ambiguous}); we intersect those identifiers with the metabolites
    present in a reference genome-scale network (iJO1366), and coverage
    is the number of ions hitting at least one model metabolite.
    \emph{Interpretation:} it asks whether a stoichiometry $\mathbf{S}$ --- the
    reaction graph the GNN operates on --- can be built for the measured
    species. A metabolite absent from every known reaction has no edges and
    cannot be modelled mechanistically, so high coverage means the measured set
    sits inside a real network; the \emph{unambiguous} count (single KEGG per
    ion) flags how many identities are clean versus mass-degenerate.
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Does it generalise?} Two of the three signals hold across all
four datasets: the dynamics are \emph{low-rank} ($\mathrm{rank}_{99}=16$--$41\%$
of ions) and a large share of metabolites are core/conserved (coverage
$46$--$77\%$). The third --- temporal smoothness of $dc/dt$ --- does
\emph{not} generalise: \emph{E.\ coli}, \emph{B.\ subtilis} and the hybrid are
noise-dominated (median lag-1 autocorrelation $0.24$--$0.41$), whereas
\textbf{\emph{Y.\ lipolytica} is smooth} (median $0.62$, $59\%$ of ions above
$0.5$). The actionable consequence: the most promising target for an actual
real-data fit is \emph{not} \emph{E.\ coli} (best network coverage but
noisiest) but \textbf{\emph{Y.\ lipolytica}} --- smooth, learnable dynamics
that are still low-rank and network-mappable. The hybrid is least amenable
(lowest coverage, noisy), though most strongly low-rank. A real fit should
therefore start from \emph{Y.\ lipolytica}, restricted to its smooth,
high-variance, network-mapped ions and judged by held-out prediction --- not by
mechanism recovery across the whole noisy measurement.
